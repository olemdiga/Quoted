Goodhart's Law

When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure

When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.

Any observed statistical regularity will tend to collapse once pressure is placed upon it for control purposes.

Metrics become useless when they become goals. People optimize what you measure, not what you value.

The moment you tie rewards to a metric, you guarantee that metric will be gamed.

What gets measured gets managed, but what gets targeted gets manipulated.

Goodhart's Law is the reason performance reviews often reduce actual performance.

Campbell's Law adds: the more any quantitative social indicator is used for decision-making, the more subject it will be to corruption pressures.

Standardized tests measure nothing except how well someone has been trained to take standardized tests.

KPI stands for Killing Performance Inevitably when used as targets rather than indicators.

The map is not the territory, and the metric is not the reality it attempts to describe.

Every metric is a proxy. When you optimize the proxy, you abandon the underlying value.

Goodhart's Law explains why Soviet factories produced nails by the ton rather than by quality - they optimized for weight.

Call center metrics create operators who hang up on customers because call duration is measured.

What you measure you get more of - but rarely the thing you actually wanted more of.

The most dangerous metrics are those that are easiest to measure but furthest from what actually matters.

Goodhart's Law is the immune response of complex systems to simplistic control mechanisms.

Any metric that becomes a target will eventually be maximized at the expense of everything that matters but isn't measured.

The only way to prevent metric manipulation is to use multiple overlapping measures and rotate them frequently.

Metrics should inform decisions, not replace judgment. When they replace judgment, they destroy value.

Goodhart's Law is the reason AI alignment is hard: you can't specify exactly what you want without creating perverse incentives.

The more precisely you define success, the more precisely you enable failure.

Goodhart's Law is the iron law of bureaucracy: procedures designed to ensure quality become obstacles to quality.

Any system of measurement will be gamed by those whose incentives depend on the measurement.

The antidote to Goodhart's Law is constant vigilance and willingness to change metrics before they're fully gamed.

Goodhart's Law reminds us that the finger pointing at the moon is not the moon.
