Sunk Cost Fallacy

The trap of honoring past investment over future value

What you've already lost is gone. The only question that matters now: do you want to lose more?

The sunk cost fallacy is the voice that says "I've come this far" when you should be asking "Is this still where I want to go?"

You stay in bad degrees, bad relationships, bad jobs, and bad investments not because they serve you, but because you've already paid for them.

Time, money, and effort already spent are gone forever. They cannot be recovered. They should not be factors in your next decision.

The most expensive words in the English language are "I've already invested too much to quit."

Every moment you remain in a losing position because of past investment, you compound the loss.

The sunk cost fallacy is why you finish bad books, sit through terrible movies, and eat food you don't want. You paid for it, so you consume it, paying twice.

Pride is the enforcer of the sunk cost fallacy. Admitting a loss feels like admitting a mistake. It is not. It is admitting reality.

The gambler who keeps betting to "win back" what he lost is the sunk cost fallacy in its purest form. The house counts on this.

You cannot get the last ten years back. You can only decide what to do with the next ten.

The sunk cost fallacy is a loyalty to a past version of yourself who made a different decision under different information.

Walking away is not failure. It is the recognition that the conditions have changed and the original thesis no longer holds.

Every resource you continue to pour into a failing endeavor is a resource stolen from a future success.

The sunk cost fallacy makes you a prisoner of your own history. The key is the willingness to accept that the past is unchangeable.

Investors know: you don't average down on a loser unless the fundamentals have changed. The same applies to life.

The most liberated people are those who can walk away from a bad investment—whether of money, time, or love—without looking back.

You are not your past decisions. You are the person making the next decision.

The sunk cost fallacy is the reason we throw good money after bad, good time after wasted time, good love after dead love.

Ask not "What have I already put in?" Ask only "What will I get out if I continue?"

The past is a sunk cost. The future is an open position. Trade accordingly.
