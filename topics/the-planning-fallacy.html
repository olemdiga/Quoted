The Planning Fallacy

Why your time estimates are always wrong and what to do about it

You systematically underestimate how long things will take. This is not a character flaw. It is a cognitive bias baked into human prediction.

The planning fallacy is the tendency to imagine best-case scenarios while ignoring the statistical reality of similar past endeavors.

Whatever number you just thought of, multiply it by two point five. Then add a buffer. Then double it again. Now you're closer to reality.

Optimism is the engine of action. The planning fallacy is the pothole it drives into.

You are not bad at estimating time. You are bad at estimating the number of things that will go wrong.

The planning fallacy persists because we rarely keep honest records of our failed predictions. We forget the overruns and remember the rare occasions we finished early.

Inside your own head, the project is already complete. The gap between internal model and external execution is where all the extra time goes.

The cure for the planning fallacy is not pessimism. It is reference class forecasting: look at how long similar projects actually took, not how long you imagine yours will take.

Every project has unknown unknowns. You cannot schedule for them because you do not know they exist. But you can schedule for the fact that they will appear.

The planning fallacy is strongest when the stakes are highest. The more you care about a project, the more optimistic your timeline becomes.

Tomorrow you will not be more productive than today you. That is a fiction invented by today you to avoid facing the current workload.

The planning fallacy is why software projects are always late, why renovations always cost more, and why you always finish your taxes on April 14th.

You imagine the path as a straight line from here to done. Reality is a maze with locked doors, missing keys, and unexpected staircases.

Break the project into the smallest possible units. Estimate those. Add them up. Now multiply by the fudge factor you still refuse to believe applies to you. It applies.

The planning fallacy is not cured by experience. Experts are just as susceptible as novices. The only cure is an external feedback loop that slaps you with reality.

When estimating, ask yourself: what is the worst-case scenario that is still plausible? Now plan for that. You will still be late, but less late.

The planning fallacy is the shadow cast by the Dunning-Kruger effect. You don't know enough to know how long it will take.

Time is the only resource you cannot recover. The planning fallacy is the tax you pay for pretending otherwise.

Your brain is a terrible project manager. Outsource the timeline to a spreadsheet and a calendar. They have no ego and no optimism.

The planning fallacy is why the last ten percent of any project takes fifty percent of the time. The finish line is always further than it appears.
