Dunning-Kruger Effect

The less you know, the more confident you are; the more you know, the more you doubt

The Dunning-Kruger effect is a cognitive bias where people with low ability at a task overestimate their ability.

Ignorance more frequently begets confidence than does knowledge.

The trouble with the world is that the stupid are cocksure and the intelligent are full of doubt.

The Dunning-Kruger effect explains why amateurs think they're experts and experts think they're amateurs.

Confidence is not a reliable indicator of competence. Often, it's the opposite.

You don't know what you don't know, and worse, you don't know how much you don't know.

The first peak of the Dunning-Kruger curve is Mount Stupid - where confidence is highest and knowledge is lowest.

True expertise includes knowing the boundaries of your expertise. Amateurs see no boundaries.

The Dunning-Kruger effect is why social media is filled with people confidently explaining things they just learned yesterday.

The more you learn about a subject, the more you realize how much there is to know - and how little you actually know.

The Dunning-Kruger effect is the cognitive equivalent of a toddler with a plastic sword thinking they can defeat a knight.

Real knowledge is quiet. False knowledge is loud.

The Dunning-Kruger effect explains why incompetent people get promoted: they confidently project competence they don't possess.

Wisdom begins when you realize how little you know. The Dunning-Kruger effect is the obstacle to that realization.

The valley of despair follows Mount Stupid - that's where real learning happens, and confidence hits bottom.

To avoid the Dunning-Kruger effect, assume you're probably wrong about things you're very confident about.

Confidence should be earned through competence, not borrowed from ignorance.

The Dunning-Kruger effect is why debates rarely change minds: both sides think they know more than they do.

True masters are perpetual students. They've climbed past Mount Stupid and never went back.

The Dunning-Kruger effect is humbling once you recognize it in yourself - which is the first step to overcoming it.

The opposite of Dunning-Kruger is intellectual humility: the awareness that your knowledge is always incomplete.

Confidence without competence is the signature of the novice who hasn't yet been humbled by complexity.

The Dunning-Kruger effect is not about intelligence; it's about metacognition - the ability to evaluate your own thinking.

Experts sound uncertain because they see nuance. Amateurs sound certain because they see only black and white.

The cure for the Dunning-Kruger effect is not more knowledge but more humility about what you don't know.
