The Peter Principle

People rise to their level of incompetence

In a hierarchy, every employee tends to rise to their level of incompetence.

The Peter Principle explains why so many managers seem incapable: they were promoted because they were good at their previous job, not this one.

Competence at one level is not competence at the next. The skills that got you here won't get you there.

The Peter Principle is why organizations are filled with people who have risen exactly one level beyond their abilities.

Work is accomplished by those employees who have not yet reached their level of incompetence.

The best engineer often becomes the worst manager - not because they're not smart, but because the skills don't transfer.

The Peter Principle operates silently: you only discover your level of incompetence after you've arrived there.

Hierarchies are machines for converting competent workers into incompetent managers.

The Peter Principle is not a bug in organizational design; it's an inevitable feature of promotion-based systems.

Super-competence is more dangerous than incompetence because it threatens the hierarchy itself.

The only way to avoid the Peter Principle is to stop promoting people based on past performance and start evaluating future potential.

The Peter Principle explains why consultants are hired: to fix problems created by people who were promoted beyond their competence.

In time, every post tends to be occupied by an employee who is incompetent to carry out its duties.

The Peter Principle is the reason "failing upward" exists: you can be incompetent and still get promoted if you're good at office politics.

The corollary to the Peter Principle: the cream doesn't always rise; sometimes it curdles at the top.

The Peter Principle teaches humility: today's star performer is tomorrow's struggling manager.

Organizations would function better if they allowed people to stay in roles they excel at rather than pushing them upward.

The Peter Principle is why "managing up" is as important as managing down: your boss may have already reached their level of incompetence.

The irony of the Peter Principle: the more competent you are, the faster you rise to your level of incompetence.

The Peter Principle doesn't mean people are stupid; it means hierarchies are stupid in how they allocate talent.

The antidote to the Peter Principle is lateral moves, skill-based advancement, and the freedom to step back without shame.

The Peter Principle reveals that promotion is often a punishment disguised as a reward.

Every organization is a pyramid of competence with a capstone of incompetence at every level.

The Peter Principle is the invisible hand that guides all bureaucratic appointments.

Understanding the Peter Principle doesn't prevent it; it just helps you recognize it when it happens to you.
